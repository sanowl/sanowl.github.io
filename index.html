  • Attention Is All You Need
    Vaswani et al., 2017
    TransformersNLPDeep Learning
    My Take: Changed NLP! Ditching recurrence for self-attention was revolutionary.

    Abstract Summary

    The dominant sequence transduction models are based on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks in an encoder-decoder architecture. The best performing models also connect the encoder and decoder through an attention mechanism. We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely. Experiments on two machine translation tasks show these models to be superior in quality while being more parallelizable and requiring significantly less time to train.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The core is the scaled dot-product attention:

    $$ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V $$

    They also introduced Multi-Head Attention, allowing the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. An attention head computes:

    $$ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V) $$

    where $W_i^Q, W_i^K, W_i^V$ are learned projection matrices. The outputs of the heads are concatenated and projected again.

  • Generative Adversarial Nets
    Goodfellow et al., 2014
    Generative ModelsDeep Learning
    My Take: GANs! Two networks in a minimax game. Unlocked so much creativity.

    Abstract Summary

    We propose a new framework for estimating generative models via an adversarial process, in which we simultaneously train two models: a generative model G that captures the data distribution, and a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than G. The training procedure for G is to maximize the probability of D making a mistake. This framework corresponds to a minimax two-player game. In the space of arbitrary functions G and D, a unique solution exists, with G recovering the training data distribution and D equal to 1/2 everywhere.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The minimax game objective function is central:

    $$ \min_G \max_D V(D,G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))] $$

    The generator $G(z; \theta_g)$ maps noise $z$ to data space, and the discriminator $D(x; \theta_d)$ outputs the probability that $x$ is real. Training involves alternating updates to $D$ (to better distinguish real from fake) and $G$ (to better fool $D$).

  • BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding
    Devlin, Chang, Lee & Toutanova, 2018
    NLPTransformersBERT
    My Take: BERT showed the power of pre-training large Transformer models on massive text corpora. The bidirectional nature using Masked Language Model (MLM) was key.

    Abstract Summary

    BERT stands for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. Unlike previous models, BERT is designed to pre-train deep bidirectional representations from unlabeled text by jointly conditioning on both left and right context in all layers. As a result, the pre-trained BERT model can be fine-tuned with just one additional output layer to create state-of-the-art models for a wide range of tasks, such as question answering and language inference, without substantial task-specific architecture modifications.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    BERT uses two pre-training tasks:

    1. Masked Language Model (MLM): Randomly masks some percentage of input tokens, and the objective is to predict the original vocabulary id of the masked word based only on its context. This allows for bidirectional context learning. Example: `Input: the man went to the [MASK] store. Labels: [MASK] = grocery`

    2. Next Sentence Prediction (NSP): Predicts whether two sentences A and B are consecutive in the original text. This helps BERT understand sentence relationships. The input representation for BERT involves summing Token Embeddings, Segment Embeddings (to distinguish sentences A and B), and Position Embeddings.

  • Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition
    He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, 2015
    Deep LearningCNNsResNet
    My Take: ResNets tackled vanishing gradients in very deep networks with residual blocks $H(x) = F(x) + x$.

    Abstract Summary

    Deeper neural networks are more difficult to train. We present a residual learning framework to ease the training of networks that are substantially deeper than those used previously. We explicitly reformulate the layers as learning residual functions with reference to the layer inputs, instead of learning unreferenced functions. We provide comprehensive empirical evidence showing that these residual networks are easier to optimize, and can gain accuracy from considerably increased depth.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The core idea is the "shortcut connection" or "skip connection". If the desired underlying mapping is $H(x)$, the stacked non-linear layers are made to fit another mapping $F(x) := H(x) - x$. The original mapping is then recast into $F(x) + x$. This formulation is realized by feedforward neural networks with shortcut connections. These connections simply perform identity mapping, and their outputs are added to the outputs of the stacked layers. A building block is defined as:

    $$ y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + x $$

    The dimensions of $x$ and $F$ must be equal. If not, a linear projection $W_s$ can be used by the shortcut to match the dimensions: $y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + W_s x$.

  • Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search
    Silver et al. (DeepMind), 2016
    Reinforcement LearningDeep LearningAlphaGo
    My Take: AlphaGo beating Lee Sedol was a landmark moment. Combined deep NNs with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).

    Abstract Summary

    The game of Go has long been viewed as the most challenging of classic games for artificial intelligence owing to its enormous search space and the difficulty of evaluating board positions and moves. Here we introduce a new approach to computer Go that uses ‘value networks’ to evaluate board positions and ‘policy networks’ to select moves. These deep neural networks are trained by a novel combination of supervised learning from human expert games, and reinforcement learning from games of self-play. Without any lookahead search, the neural networks play Go at the level of state-of-the-art Monte Carlo tree search programs that simulate thousands of random games of self-play. We also introduce a new search algorithm that combines Monte Carlo simulation with value and policy networks.

    Further Insights

    AlphaGo's success relied on several components:

    1. Policy Network ($p_\pi$): A convolutional network trained via supervised learning (SL) on expert human moves, then refined via reinforcement learning (RL) by playing against itself. It outputs probabilities for possible moves.

    2. Value Network ($v_\theta$): Another convolutional network trained to predict the outcome (win/loss) from a given board position. This was trained on data generated from self-play games by the RL policy network.

    3. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS): MCTS uses the policy network to guide its simulations (reducing breadth of search) and the value network to evaluate positions (reducing depth of search). The action selection in MCTS considers both exploration and exploitation.

  • The Foundation of the General Theory of Relativity
    Einstein, 1916
    General RelativityGravity
    My Take: Einstein's masterpiece. Gravity as spacetime curvature.

    Summary

    This paper lays out the complete formulation of the general theory of relativity, where gravity is described as a manifestation of the curvature of spacetime. It generalizes special relativity and Newton's law of universal gravitation, providing a unified description of gravity as a geometric property of space and time.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The Einstein Field Equations (EFE) are the core:

    $$ R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}R g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu} $$

    Where:

    • $R_{\mu\nu}$ is the Ricci curvature tensor.
    • $R$ is the scalar curvature.
    • $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the metric tensor.
    • $\Lambda$ is the cosmological constant.
    • $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant.
    • $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum.
    • $T_{\mu\nu}$ is the stress-energy tensor, which describes the density and flux of energy and momentum in spacetime. For example, for a perfect fluid, $T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p/c^2)u_\mu u_\nu + p g_{\mu\nu}$.

    The EFE is a set of 10 non-linear partial differential equations. Their solutions describe various gravitational phenomena, such as gravitational waves, black holes, and the expansion of the universe.

  • Quantisation as an Eigenvalue Problem
    Schrödinger, 1926
    Quantum MechanicsSchrödinger Equation
    My Take: The Schrödinger equation! Bedrock of non-relativistic QM.

    Summary

    In this series of papers, Schrödinger introduced his wave equation, providing a description of quantum mechanics in terms of wave functions. This formulation was an alternative to Heisenberg's matrix mechanics but was later shown to be equivalent. It describes how the quantum state of a physical system changes over time.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE):

    $$ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \hat{H}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) $$

    The time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE), for systems where the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ is not time-dependent, is derived by separation of variables, $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r})e^{-iEt/\hbar}$:

    $$ \hat{H}\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\psi(\mathbf{r}) $$

    This is an eigenvalue equation, where $E$ are the energy eigenvalues and $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ are the energy eigenfunctions (stationary states). The Hamiltonian operator for a single particle in a potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ is often:

    $$ \hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r}) $$

    The expectation value of an observable $A$ (represented by an operator $\hat{A}$) is given by $\langle A \rangle = \int \Psi^* \hat{A} \Psi \, d\tau$.

  • Broken Symmetry and the Mass of Gauge Bosons
    Higgs, 1964
    Particle PhysicsHiggs Mechanism
    My Take: Explained how particles acquire mass via spontaneous symmetry breaking. The potential $V(\phi) = -\mu^2 |\phi|^2 + \lambda |\phi|^4$ is key.

    Abstract Summary (Conceptual)

    This paper (and others by Brout & Englert; Guralnik, Hagen & Kibble) proposed a mechanism by which fundamental particles, particularly gauge bosons in gauge theories, can acquire mass. It involves a scalar field (the Higgs field) that permeates all space and has a non-zero vacuum expectation value. Interactions with this field give particles their mass. This resolved a major issue in the formulation of the Standard Model of particle physics, where gauge invariance initially implied massless gauge bosons.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The Higgs mechanism involves a scalar field $\phi$ with a "mexican hat" potential:

    $$ V(\phi) = -\frac{1}{2}\mu^2 |\phi|^2 + \frac{1}{4}\lambda (|\phi|^2)^2 $$

    When $\mu^2 > 0$, the minimum of the potential is not at $\phi=0$, but at $|\phi_0|^2 = \frac{\mu^2}{\lambda} = v^2$. This non-zero vacuum expectation value $v$ breaks the electroweak symmetry spontaneously. When gauge bosons (like W and Z bosons) couple to this Higgs field, they acquire a mass term in the Lagrangian. For example, for a U(1) gauge boson $A_\mu$ coupling to a complex scalar $\phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_1 + i\phi_2)$, if $\phi_1$ acquires a VEV $v$, the kinetic term for $\phi$ $(D_\mu \phi)^\dagger (D^\mu \phi)$ where $D_\mu = \partial_\mu - igA_\mu$, yields a mass term for $A_\mu$ of the form $\frac{1}{2} g^2 v^2 A_\mu A^\mu$. The Higgs boson itself is the quantum excitation of the Higgs field around its VEV.

  • Theory of Superconductivity
    Bardeen, Cooper & Schrieffer, 1957
    Condensed MatterSuperconductivity
    My Take: BCS Theory explained superconductivity via Cooper pairs of electrons, leading to an energy gap $\Delta$.

    Abstract Summary

    This theory explains conventional superconductivity as a microscopic effect caused by a condensation of Cooper pairs into a boson-like state. These Cooper pairs consist of two electrons that are indirectly attracted to each other through their interaction with the crystal lattice (phonons). This pairing opens up an energy gap in the density of states at the Fermi level, which is crucial for the properties of superconductors, such as zero electrical resistance and the Meissner effect.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    Key concepts include:

    1. Cooper Pairs: Two electrons with opposite momenta and spins can form a bound state (a Cooper pair) due to an attractive interaction mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons), even if the direct Coulomb interaction is repulsive.

    2. Energy Gap ($\Delta$): The formation of Cooper pairs leads to an energy gap $\Delta$ in the electronic excitation spectrum at the Fermi energy $E_F$. The energy of an excitation (quasiparticle) is $E_k = \sqrt{\epsilon_k^2 + \Delta^2}$, where $\epsilon_k$ is the single-particle energy relative to $E_F$. This gap prevents small-energy scattering events that would normally cause resistance.

    3. BCS Ground State Wavefunction: The ground state is a coherent superposition of states with different numbers of Cooper pairs. The critical temperature $T_c$ below which superconductivity occurs is related to the energy gap by $2\Delta(0) \approx 3.53 k_B T_c$ at $T=0$. The gap $\Delta$ itself depends on temperature and vanishes at $T_c$.

  • A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems
    Kalman, 1960
    Control TheoryEstimationKalman Filter
    My Take: The Kalman Filter! Essential for state estimation.

    Summary

    This paper introduced an optimal recursive data processing algorithm, the Kalman filter. It's used to estimate the state of a linear dynamic system from a series of noisy measurements. It operates by propagating the mean and covariance of the state estimate forward in time based on a system model (prediction step) and then updating this estimate using new measurements (update step).

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The Kalman filter operates in a two-step process:

    1. Prediction Step:

    Predicted state estimate: $\hat{x}_k^- = A \hat{x}_{k-1} + B u_{k-1}$

    Predicted error covariance: $P_k^- = A P_{k-1} A^T + Q$

    2. Update Step:

    Kalman Gain: $K_k = P_k^- H^T (H P_k^- H^T + R)^{-1}$

    Updated state estimate: $\hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k (z_k - H \hat{x}_k^-)$

    Updated error covariance: $P_k = (I - K_k H) P_k^-$

    Where: $\hat{x}$ is the state estimate, $P$ is the error covariance, $A$ is the state transition matrix, $B$ is the control input matrix, $u$ is the control input, $Q$ is the process noise covariance, $H$ is the observation matrix, $R$ is the measurement noise covariance, $z_k$ is the measurement at time $k$, and $K_k$ is the Kalman gain.

  • Real-Time Obstacle Avoidance for Manipulators and Mobile Robots
    Khatib, 1986
    Motion PlanningPotential Fields
    My Take: Potential fields for path planning! Robot pulled to goal, pushed from obstacles. Elegant.

    Abstract Summary

    This paper presents the artificial potential field approach for real-time obstacle avoidance for manipulators and mobile robots. The robot moves in a field of forces, where it is attracted to its goal configuration and repelled by obstacles. The resulting motion is generated by the sum of these forces, allowing for smooth, reactive path generation.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The total potential field $U(q)$ at a configuration $q$ is the sum of an attractive potential $U_{att}(q)$ pulling the robot towards the goal $q_{goal}$, and a repulsive potential $U_{rep}(q)$ pushing the robot away from obstacles.

    $$ U(q) = U_{att}(q) + U_{rep}(q) $$

    A common form for the attractive potential is parabolic:

    $$ U_{att}(q) = \frac{1}{2} \zeta \rho^2(q, q_{goal}) $$

    where $\zeta$ is a positive scaling factor and $\rho(q, q_{goal})$ is the distance to the goal.

    A common form for the repulsive potential from an obstacle is:

    $$ U_{rep}(q) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} \eta \left(\frac{1}{\rho(q, q_{obs})} - \frac{1}{\rho_0}\right)^2 & \text{if } \rho(q, q_{obs}) \le \rho_0 \\ 0 & \text{if } \rho(q, q_{obs}) > \rho_0 \end{cases} $$

    where $\eta$ is a positive scaling factor, $\rho(q, q_{obs})$ is the minimum distance to the obstacle, and $\rho_0$ is the distance of influence of the obstacle.

    The "force" applied to the robot is the negative gradient of the total potential: $F(q) = -\nabla U(q)$. This force can then be used to control the robot's velocity or acceleration.

    A major challenge with potential fields is the problem of local minima, where the robot can get stuck even if a path to the goal exists.

  • STRIPS: A New Approach to the Application of Theorem Proving to Problem Solving
    Fikes & Nilsson, 1971
    AI PlanningRoboticsSTRIPS
    My Take: STRIPS was a foundational planner, used in Shakey. Represented states and actions logically.

    Abstract Summary

    This paper describes the STRIPS (Stanford Research Institute Problem Solver) system, a problem solver designed for controlling autonomous robots like Shakey. STRIPS represents the world as a set of first-order logic formulas and actions as operators that modify this state. It uses a means-ends analysis approach combined with theorem proving to find a sequence of actions to achieve a goal state from an initial state.

    Further Insights

    Key components of STRIPS representation:

    1. State Representation: A state is represented by a set of ground literals (positive atomic formulas) that are true in that state. Anything not listed is assumed false (Closed World Assumption).

    2. Goal Representation: A goal is also represented by a set of ground literals. The system aims to find a state where all goal literals are true.

    3. Action Representation (Operators): Each action is defined by:

    • Action Name: e.g., `pickup(X)`
    • Preconditions: A set of literals that must be true for the action to be applicable. e.g., `on_table(X), clear(X), hand_empty`.
    • Add List: A set of literals that become true after the action is performed. e.g., `holding(X)`.
    • Delete List: A set of literals that become false (are removed from the state) after the action. e.g., `on_table(X), clear(X), hand_empty`.

    STRIPS uses a search algorithm (means-ends analysis) where it tries to reduce the differences between the current state and the goal state by selecting applicable operators. It was a significant step in automated planning and influenced many subsequent planning systems.

  • Estimating Uncertain Spatial Relationships in Robotics
    Smith, Self & Cheeseman, 1990
    SLAMProbabilistic RoboticsEKF SLAM
    My Take: Seminal for SLAM, especially EKF SLAM. Representing map and robot pose with uncertainties (covariances) was key.

    Abstract Summary (Conceptual)

    This paper (and related works like "Representing and Estimating Spatial Uncertainty" by Smith and Cheeseman, 1986) laid crucial groundwork for Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM). It focused on how to represent and manage uncertainty in the spatial relationships between a robot and features in its environment. The key idea was to use stochastic maps, where the positions of features and the robot's own pose are described by probability distributions (typically Gaussians, characterized by means and covariance matrices). This allowed for a mathematically rigorous way to fuse sensor information and update the map and robot pose over time.

    Further Insights & Key Math

    The core of EKF-SLAM involves augmenting the robot's state vector $x_v$ with the locations of all observed map features $m_i$: $x_a = [x_v^T, m_1^T, m_2^T, ..., m_N^T]^T$. The uncertainty is captured in a large covariance matrix $P_a$.

    Prediction Step (Robot Motion):

    When the robot moves, its pose estimate $\hat{x}_v$ is updated using a motion model $f(\cdot)$, and its covariance $P_{vv}$ is updated: $$ \hat{x}_v(k|k-1) = f(\hat{x}_v(k-1|k-1), u(k)) $$ $$ P_{vv}(k|k-1) = \nabla_x f P_{vv}(k-1|k-1) \nabla_x f^T + Q(k) $$ Map feature estimates and their covariances (and cross-covariances with the robot) are also propagated.

    Update Step (Observation of a Feature):

    When a feature $m_j$ is observed with measurement $z_j$, the innovation (difference between expected and actual measurement) is $ \nu_j = z_j - h(\hat{x}_v(k|k-1), \hat{m}_j(k|k-1)) $. The innovation covariance is $S_j = \nabla h P_a(k|k-1) \nabla h^T + R_j$. The Kalman gain is $K_j = P_a(k|k-1) \nabla h^T S_j^{-1}$. The state vector and covariance matrix are updated: $$ \hat{x}_a(k|k) = \hat{x}_a(k|k-1) + K_j \nu_j $$ $$ P_a(k|k) = (I - K_j \nabla h) P_a(k|k-1) $$

    A key challenge was managing the growing size and computational complexity of the covariance matrix $P_a$ and correctly handling data association (matching observations to existing map features).
